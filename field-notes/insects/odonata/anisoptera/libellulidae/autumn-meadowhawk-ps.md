### [*Sympetrum vicinum*](/field-notes/insects/odonata/anisoptera/libellulidae/sympetrum-vicinum/)
{: .taxonomy-heading .taxonomy-last}

The [**Autumn Meadowhawk**](/gallery/animals/insects/dragonflies/autumn-meadowhawk/) photographs collected here follow several low-perch views in meadow and understory vegetation: a curled leaf, a dry diagonal stem, a broad green leaf, a close face view, a dorsal view, and a grass-blade perch. For identification, habitat, and natural-history details, see the [*Sympetrum vicinum*](/field-notes/insects/odonata/anisoptera/libellulidae/sympetrum-vicinum/) species page.

Taken separately, the photographs are modest late-season dragonfly portraits. Together, they show how much the character of an Autumn Meadowhawk image changes with angle, light, and perch. A small red-orange skimmer can become a leaf-edge portrait, a warm ember on a dry stem, or a nearly hidden shape among green blades.

---

## Shadowlight in the Leaves

The strongest image in the set is **E22A6674**, where the meadowhawk rests along a curled leaf edge. The dark background gives the face and near wing better separation, while the pale face, reddish eyes, and fine wing venation remain readable.

**D79A4948** carries a similar mood from a different angle. The broad leaf and deep shade make the image feel more like an understory encounter than an open meadow portrait. It is less direct than the newer frame, but the warm amber body against the shaded green still gives it a useful place in the sequence.

**D79A0209** works more as context. The red body is clear against the broad green leaf, and the full abdomen remains visible, but the bright leaf surface and spotted texture compete more strongly with the subject. It still helps document the low, leafy perch setting.

## Close Portrait and Dorsal View

**D79A0216** brings the viewer closest to the face and wing structure. The abdomen is cropped out, so the image does not work as a complete field-mark view, but the reddish eyes, pale face, dark legs, wing venation, and red pterostigmata make it valuable as a detail study.

**E22A6681** moves to the opposite viewpoint. From above, the slender abdomen and low perch among wet leaves are easier to see. The surrounding vegetation is busier, but the dorsal angle adds visual context that the face portraits do not provide.

## Oak-Leaf Perches

A later encounter found an Autumn Meadowhawk using low oak leaves as repeated perches. These frames are better suited to the Photographic Sequence page than the main Gallery, but they show useful behavior and setting: the dragonfly settling into layered foliage, nearly disappearing among sunlit greens, dark gaps, reflective leaf surfaces, and jagged leaf edges.

The EF100-400mm lens worked well for most of this sequence because the longer working distance allowed a fuller view of the perch setting without crowding the subject. The result is less intimate than the close macro views, but it records how the meadowhawk used the oak leaves as low lookout points.

## Perspective

The Autumn Meadowhawk page began with older photographs that felt more useful than exciting. They documented the species, but several of them were limited by bright leaf surfaces, busy vegetation, or partial obstruction. The later **E22A6674** image changed the balance of the page by giving the set a stronger lead: the same low-leaf setting, but with cleaner separation, better facial detail, and a more atmospheric background.

The sequence is strongest when the photographs are not treated only as ranked images. The best portrait leads, the dry-stem view adds season and perch structure, the close portrait shows the face and wing venation, and the dorsal and grass-blade views broaden the record of how these small dragonflies use low vegetation.

The result is a progression:

**shadowed leaf portrait → dry-stem perch → understory light → face detail → broad-leaf context → dorsal view → grass-blade field note**
{: .caption}

That progression also explains why some photographs belong here even if they are not strong enough to lead the Gallery page. In the Field Notes section, their value comes from context, comparison, and the way they show the meadowhawk from different angles.
