## *Tyrannus tyrannus*
{: .taxonomy-heading}

### Family: [Tyrannidae](/field-notes/birds/passeriformes/tyrannidae/)
{: .taxonomy-heading .taxonomy-last}

[**Eastern Kingbird**](/gallery/animals/birds/perching/eastern-kingbird/) (*Tyrannus tyrannus*) is a medium-sized migratory flycatcher of open country, woodland edges, wetlands, and other habitats that combine open hunting space with exposed perches. Its dark head and upperparts contrast sharply with clean white underparts, while a crisp white terminal band across the dark tail provides one of the species' strongest field marks.

For representative images and visual context, see the page: [**Eastern Kingbird**](/gallery/animals/birds/perching/eastern-kingbird/) in the **Gallery** section.

## Description

*Tyrannus tyrannus* is recognized by:

- **Size:** medium-sized, sturdy songbird, approximately 7.5–9.1 in (19–23 cm)  
- **Upperparts:** dark gray to blackish, with the head generally darker than the back and wings  
- **Underparts:** clean white from the throat through the breast and belly  
- **Tail:** dark, broad, and nearly square-tipped with a conspicuous white terminal band  
- **Bill:** relatively short, straight, and broad-based, suited to catching insects in flight  
- **Shape:** large-headed and broad-shouldered, often holding an upright posture on exposed perches  
- **Crown patch:** small red, orange, or yellow feathers may be present on the crown but are normally concealed  

Males and females are very similar in outward appearance and usually cannot be sexed reliably from normal field views alone.

## Habitat and Range

Typical breeding habitats include:

- Open fields and meadows  
- Pastures and grasslands  
- Woodland and forest edges  
- Wetland and marsh margins  
- Roadsides, shelterbelts, and scattered trees  
- Open areas near ponds, lakes, and rivers  

Notes may include:

- A summer breeding species in Minnesota  
- Frequently uses exposed branches, treetops, fences, and utility lines as hunting perches  
- Requires open airspace for aerial insect hunting while retaining nearby trees or shrubs for nesting and lookout sites  
- Migrates long distances and spends the winter in South America  
- Often conspicuous during the breeding season because of its preference for exposed perches

## Identification

**Key features for field diagnosis:**

- **Head and back:** dark gray to blackish, with the head usually appearing darkest  
- **Underparts:** sharply contrasting white throat, breast, and belly  
- **Tail:** broad dark tail ending in a crisp white terminal band  
- **Posture:** upright and alert, often on an exposed perch  
- **Bill:** relatively short and straight compared with many other large flycatchers  
- **Voice:** sharp, buzzy, or metallic calls often given from exposed positions  
- **Behavior:** repeatedly sallies from a perch to capture flying insects, often returning to the same or a nearby lookout  

The white terminal band across the tail is especially useful. It differs from the white outer tail feathers of the Western Kingbird, which also has a paler gray head and yellow underparts. Eastern Phoebe lacks the white tail tip and is smaller and more subdued in color, while Great Crested Flycatcher shows yellow underparts and conspicuous rufous tones in the wings and tail.

## Behavior and Ecology

- Hunts primarily by watching from an exposed perch and flying out after insects  
- Often returns to the same branch, treetop, fence, or wire after a short aerial pursuit  
- Flight while hunting can be quick and direct, with abrupt changes in direction  
- Strongly territorial during the breeding season and may aggressively chase much larger birds  
- Diet consists primarily of:
  - **Flying insects and other arthropods during the breeding season**  
  - **Berries and other fruit, especially during migration and on the wintering grounds**  
- Nests are usually placed in trees or large shrubs within relatively open habitats  
- Both adults participate in feeding the young  
- Pairs may repeatedly use familiar lookout points and flight paths within a breeding territory

## Field Observation

On August 8, 2026, a pair of Eastern Kingbirds was observed sharing an exposed diagonal branch beneath a clear morning sky. Both birds remained close together through a short photographic sequence, providing unusually clear views of their posture, feather arrangement, and interaction on the perch.

One bird briefly shifted from a conventional perched position into a more animated posture. The tail opened into a broad fan while the visible wing feathers separated into a smooth curving array. The progression of the flight feathers produced a changing arc rather than a simple straight-edged fan, making the feather structure particularly noticeable against the blue sky.

The sequence also shows subtle changes in the bird's footing. In E22A8177, several toes and claws are especially visible around the branch. By E22A8175, one foot appears to carry most of the bird's weight while the opposite foot remains only lightly engaged with the perch. These small changes suggest that the wing and tail spread occurred during a brief shift in balance rather than during a completely static pose.

E22A8176 provides the strongest view of the two birds together, with catchlights visible in both eyes. E22A8175 combines the strongest eye detail with the most graceful wing and tail arrangement, while E22A8173 records a more energetic stage of the feather spread.

Because male and female Eastern Kingbirds are very similar in plumage, the photographs alone do not provide enough evidence to confidently assign a sex to either bird.

## Notes

- The white terminal tail band is one of the most dependable field marks, particularly when the tail is spread or seen in flight  
- The darker head can create a hooded appearance against the somewhat grayer back and wings  
- The normally concealed crown patch should not be expected during routine identification  
- Exposed branches and treetops are useful places to watch because the bird may repeatedly return after aerial insect pursuits  
- A perched bird may appear compact and relatively restrained until the wing or tail opens, revealing considerably more feather structure  
- Short photographic bursts can be especially useful for studying subtle changes in wing position, tail spread, balance, and foot placement  
- Sex should generally remain unspecified unless behavior or other evidence provides stronger support than plumage alone
