## Tyrannidae (Tyrant Flycatchers)

**Tyrannidae** is a family of primarily insect-eating birds of the Americas that includes kingbirds, phoebes, pewees, and many other flycatchers. Members of this family are often recognized by their relatively broad-based bills, upright posture, alert behavior, and characteristic habit of watching from a perch before flying out to capture insects.

They occupy a wide variety of habitats, from open grasslands and wetlands to woodland edges, shrublands, and forest interiors. Many species depend on a combination of suitable perches and open airspace for hunting.

In the field, behavior, posture, voice, and habitat are often as important as plumage for identification.

## General Characteristics

- **Size:** Small to medium-sized songbirds, ranging from compact flycatchers to larger, broad-shouldered kingbirds
- **Bill:** Usually straight, flattened, and relatively broad at the base, adapted for capturing insects
- **Plumage:** Often gray, brown, olive, black, white, or yellow; some species are more strongly patterned than others
- **Voice:** Frequently important for identification, particularly among species with similar plumage
- **Flight:** Often quick and agile, with short aerial pursuits launched from an exposed or partially concealed perch

---

## Habitat and Range

Members of this family may be encountered in:

- **Open fields, meadows, and grasslands**
- **Wetland and marsh edges**
- **Woodland and forest edges**
- **Shrublands and brushy openings**
- **Parks, pastures, roadsides, and other areas with exposed perches**

Seasonal patterns may include:

- **Breeding habitat:** open country, woodland edges, wetlands, shrublands, or forest depending on species
- **Migration tendencies:** many species present in Minnesota are migratory and occur primarily during the breeding season or migration
- **Winter distribution:** migratory species may move into Central America, South America, or warmer portions of North America

Birds are often detected first by an exposed silhouette, a repeated call, or a sudden flight from a favored perch.

## Field Recognition

**Key features for identifying this family:**

- **Shape:** Often upright with a relatively large head, broad shoulders, and alert posture
- **Bill:** Straight and broad-based rather than conical like the bill of a sparrow or finch
- **Plumage:** Frequently subdued, making structure and behavior important identification clues
- **Movement:** Commonly watches from a perch before making a short flight to capture an insect
- **Voice:** Calls and songs can be highly diagnostic, especially among similar-looking flycatchers
- **Behavior:** Many species repeatedly return to the same perch or a nearby lookout after an aerial pursuit

The combination of perch selection, hunting behavior, voice, and structural details may be more useful than color alone.

---

## Movement and Flight

- **Flight:** Often rapid and direct during short pursuits, with abrupt turns while following flying insects
- **Perching:** Frequently upright on branches, treetops, fence lines, wires, posts, or other lookout points
- **Foraging:** Many species use a perch-and-sally strategy, flying outward to capture insects before returning to a perch
- **Display:** Territorial flights, calling from exposed positions, wing movements, and aggressive pursuits may become conspicuous during the breeding season

Movement patterns can be diagnostic. A bird repeatedly leaving an exposed perch, catching an insect in flight, and returning to the same location strongly suggests flycatcher behavior.

---

## Similar Families

This family may be confused with:

- **Turdidae:** Thrushes and bluebirds may share an upright posture, but generally have narrower bills and are more likely to forage on or near the ground
- **Mimidae:** Mockingbirds, catbirds, and thrashers may use exposed perches but typically have longer tails, different bill structure, and different foraging behavior
- **Cardinalidae:** Some buntings and grosbeaks may occupy similar woodland-edge habitats, but have thicker, more conical bills adapted for seeds
- **Parulidae:** Warblers may also pursue insects among foliage, but are generally smaller, more active within vegetation, and lack the broad-based flycatcher bill

Distinction is often based on bill shape, posture, hunting behavior, and voice rather than any single plumage feature.

## Notes

- Tyrant flycatchers are strongly associated with insect hunting, although diet and foraging methods vary among species.
- A broad-based bill is an important structural characteristic and reflects the family's emphasis on capturing insects.
- Perch-and-sally behavior is especially conspicuous in kingbirds and many other open-country flycatchers.
- Voice can be essential for distinguishing species whose plumage is otherwise similar.
- Eastern Kingbirds favor conspicuous lookout points and may repeatedly return to the same branch, treetop, fence, or wire after aerial pursuits.
- The white terminal band of the Eastern Kingbird's tail is particularly useful when the tail is spread or the bird is seen in flight.
- Male and female Eastern Kingbirds are very similar in normal field views and should not generally be assigned a sex from plumage alone.
