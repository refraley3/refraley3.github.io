## *Cistothorus stellaris*
{: .taxonomy-heading}

### Family: [Troglodytidae](/field-notes/birds/passeriformes/troglodytidae/)
{: .taxonomy-heading .taxonomy-last}

[**Sedge Wren**](/gallery/animals/birds/perching/sedge-wren/) (*Cistothorus stellaris*) is a small, secretive wren of wet meadows, sedge beds, marsh margins, and dense grassy cover. It is often detected first by its dry, chattering song or by quick movement low in the vegetation.

For representative images and visual context, see the page: [**Sedge Wren**](/gallery/animals/birds/perching/sedge-wren/) in the **Gallery** section.

---

## Description

*Cistothorus stellaris* is recognized by:

- **Size:** very small songbird, approximately 4–5 in (10–12 cm)  
- **Plumage:** warm brown and buff with pale streaking across the crown and back  
- **Face pattern:** subtle pale eyebrow with a darker line through the eye  
- **Bill:** short, slender, and pointed  
- **Wings:** patterned with dark bars and pale edging  
- **Tail:** short, distinctly barred, and frequently held upright  
- **Underparts:** pale throat and breast with warmer buff along the sides and flanks  

The streaked upperparts and barred tail are especially useful when the bird briefly rises above the surrounding vegetation.

## Habitat and Range

Typical habitats include:

- Wet meadows and sedge-dominated grasslands  
- Marsh margins and damp prairie depressions  
- Overgrown fields with dense, upright vegetation  
- Roadside wetlands and other seasonally wet grassy areas  

Notes may include:

- Present in Minnesota primarily during the breeding season  
- Often local and difficult to locate despite occurring in suitable habitat  
- Strongly associated with dense vegetation that provides concealment close to the ground  
- May appear briefly on exposed stems, shrubs, or low branches before dropping back into cover

## Identification

**Key features for field diagnosis:**

- **Crown and back:** finely streaked with pale markings against a dark brown background  
- **Wings and tail:** clearly barred  
- **Face:** faint pale eyebrow and relatively plain cheek  
- **Bill:** fine and pointed rather than thick and conical  
- **Tail posture:** usually short and cocked upward  
- **Habitat:** wet meadow, sedge, or dense grassy cover  
- **Behavior:** moves low through vegetation and often disappears completely from view  
- **Voice:** dry, mechanical chatter or trill, frequently heard before the bird is seen  

Sedge Wrens can resemble Northern House Wrens, especially when wet or fluffed. Northern House Wrens are generally plainer across the crown and back, while Sedge Wrens show stronger pale streaking above and more patterned wings and tail.

Marsh Wrens are usually associated with taller emergent marsh vegetation and tend to show a stronger pale eyebrow, richer rufous tones, and a more contrasting back pattern.

---

## Behavior and Ecology

- Moves quickly through grass, sedges, and low tangles, often below the tops of the vegetation  
- Frequently disappears into dense cover and may remain hidden even at close range  
- Rises to exposed stems or low branches to sing, watch, preen, or dry  
- Forages for insects, spiders, and other small invertebrates among stems and leaves  
- Builds a rounded or domed nest woven into dense upright vegetation  
- May use several nearby singing or lookout perches within the same patch of habitat  

The bird photographed for the Gallery page had moved through grass soaked by rain from the previous day. After repeatedly disappearing into the dense cover with other Sedge Wrens, it rose onto thorny branches with wet, separated breast feathers and remained exposed long enough to preen and dry in the morning light.

---

## Notes

- Very small size and secretive movement can make the species difficult to follow visually  
- Streaking across the crown and back is a useful distinction from the plainer Northern House Wren  
- Wet plumage may darken the bird and temporarily obscure its normal body shape and feather pattern  
- The upright barred tail remains a useful clue when the upperparts are visible  
- Habitat and voice are often as important as plumage for identification  
- Brief appearances above the grass may provide the clearest views of the face, back, wings, and tail
