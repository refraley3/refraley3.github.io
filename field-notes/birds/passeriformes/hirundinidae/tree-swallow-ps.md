### [*Tachycineta bicolor*](/field-notes/birds/passeriformes/hirundinidae/tachycineta-bicolor/)
{: .taxonomy-heading .taxonomy-last}

The [**Tree Swallow**](/gallery/animals/birds/perching/tree-swallow/) photographs collected here follow a morning encounter that moved from distant pipe-perch portraits to close studies of plumage, pair behavior, wing movement, and activity around a nest box. For identification, habitat, and natural-history details, see the [*Tachycineta bicolor*](/field-notes/birds/passeriformes/hirundinidae/tachycineta-bicolor/) species page.

Taken separately, many of the photographs are quiet portraits. Together, they show how quickly the character of a Tree Swallow changes with angle, light, posture, and distance: blue-green iridescence becomes visible, smooth feathers lift into texture, and a bird that appears settled can open a wing and seem ready for flight in an instant.

---

## In the Morning Light

The first close portraits make color the dominant subject. Fresh morning sun changes the upperparts from nearly dark to metallic blue-green as the bird turns relative to the light.

**E22A1661** gives the cleanest introductory pose. The bright white breast, dark cap, and blue-green back are separated clearly against the open sky.

**E22A1680** shifts the emphasis toward iridescence. The bird turns slightly toward the camera, bringing more connection to the portrait while the shoulder and back catch the strongest blue-green reflection.

**E22A1679** adds a different kind of presence. With the head turned back across the shoulder, the bird shows both the metallic upperparts and the lifted feather edges of the cool morning plumage.

## Ruffled Feathers

The pipe-perch sequence also revealed a softer side of a bird usually defined by streamlined flight. In the cool morning air, feathers along the shoulder and back lifted away from the body, briefly changing the swallow's shape.

**E22A1664** and **E22A1666** are supporting frames rather than primary gallery portraits, but they make the change easy to see. The upperparts lose some of their smooth aerodynamic surface and become layered and textured.

That contrast matters because the bird can look very different when settled on a perch than when it is cutting through open air.

## Paired on the Pipe

Tree Swallows are often encountered as more than isolated birds around nesting areas. **E22A1761** changes the story from a single portrait to a shared perch.

Both birds are in good focus, and the front-facing posture reveals the fine texture of the white breast plumage. The composition is quieter than the later nest-box interaction, but the pair gives the morning a social context that the single-bird portraits cannot.

## Lift Before Flight

The wing sequence captures the boundary between a perched bird and an aerial one.

**E22A1657** is the strongest action frame. One wing rises almost fully open while the bird remains anchored to the pipe, exposing the length and structure that are mostly hidden in a resting portrait.

**E22A1658** follows with the wing lowered but still extended. The body remains poised forward, retaining the sense that motion has not fully ended.

**E22A1654** returns to a quieter posture. Used with the other two frames, it completes a small progression from stillness into motion and back toward composure.

## Nest Box and Pair Behavior

Around the nest box, the swallows seem less like birds passing through open air and more like residents of a particular place. The roofline becomes a perch, lookout, meeting point, and stage for small social gestures.

**E22A1870** catches one bird with a wing lowered and the body feathers loosened. The movement feels less like a launch than a readjustment, giving the frame its title, **Ruffling at the Nest Box**.

**E22A1860** turns the interaction outward. Both birds have their bills open across the roofline, making the frame feel animated rather than simply paired. Nearby frames suggest that the more strongly iridescent bird is likely the male and the more animated bird likely the female, although the photographs alone do not make the sex distinction absolute.

**E22A1888** closes the nest-box sequence with a single bird standing above the entrance. The blue-green plumage is especially strong against the muted background, while the seasoned box and small patches of lichen keep the photograph tied to the nesting site.

## Photographer’s Perspective

The photographs on this page were not made in a single easy approach. The pipe perch stood about twenty feet from the trail, and the Tree Swallows proved wary from the beginning. I moved slowly along the trail edge rather than walking toward them directly, but even so they flushed when I had closed only part of the distance. Each time, I returned to the tripod, reviewed the images, adjusted the camera, and waited for the birds to settle back onto the pipe.

The process repeated several times through the morning. As the light strengthened, the ISO came down in stages, and with each new attempt the distance closed a little more. What began as a cautious long approach gradually became a closer and more intimate study, until some frames were made at much shorter focal lengths than I would have expected at the start. By the time **E22A1761** was taken at 100mm, the effort had become part of the photograph itself: patience, retreat, return, and the small reward of being accepted just a little closer.

That slower approach shaped the photographs as much as the light did. The sequence became more complete not only because the color grew richer and the detail cleaner, but because the birds revealed more with each return: feather texture, shared perches, wing movement, and the social energy around the nest box.

The result is less a ranked collection of individual photographs than a progression:

**light and color → feather texture → pair presence → movement → nest-box behavior**

That progression is also why the photographs do not need to appear in order of rating. A gallery can tell a stronger story when each image contributes something different.
