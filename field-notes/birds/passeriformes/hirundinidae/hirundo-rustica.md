## *Hirundo rustica*
{: .taxonomy-heading}

### Family: [Hirundinidae](/field-notes/birds/passeriformes/hirundinidae/)
{: .taxonomy-heading .taxonomy-last}

[**Barn Swallow**](/gallery/animals/birds/perching/barn-swallow/) (*Hirundo rustica*) is a slender, long-winged swallow of open country, farms, wetlands, shorelines, and other areas where flying insects are abundant. Its deeply forked tail, pointed wings, warm underparts, and reddish throat distinguish it from the other swallows commonly encountered in Minnesota.

Barn Swallows spend much of their time in the air, often flying low over fields or water while pursuing insects. When perched, their compact body and folded wings can conceal much of the structure that becomes obvious in flight or during stretching and preening.

## Description

*Hirundo rustica* is recognized by:

- **Size:** small, slender swallow with a streamlined body
- **Wings:** long, narrow, and sharply pointed
- **Tail:** deeply forked, with elongated outer tail feathers especially noticeable on adults
- **Upperparts:** glossy dark blue to blue-black on adults
- **Forehead and throat:** reddish to chestnut
- **Underparts:** warm buff to cinnamon, varying with age, sex, and light
- **Bill:** very short and broad at the base, suited to catching insects in flight
- **Legs and feet:** small relative to the body, reflecting a bird adapted primarily for aerial activity

Adult males generally have longer outer tail streamers than females, although the difference may be difficult to judge without direct comparison.

Juveniles resemble adults but usually appear softer and less strongly colored. They have shorter tail streamers, paler underparts, and may show a conspicuous yellow gape at the base of the bill.

## Habitat and Range

Typical habitats include:

- Open fields and pastures
- Agricultural areas and farmsteads
- Wetlands, ponds, lakeshores, and river corridors
- Roadsides and other open areas with abundant flying insects
- Bridges, barns, sheds, culverts, and other structures suitable for nesting

In Minnesota:

- Barn Swallows are primarily spring and summer residents
- They arrive during migration as insect activity increases
- Breeding birds are strongly associated with open feeding areas near suitable nesting structures
- They often forage over water, grasslands, fields, and roads
- Large numbers may gather before southward migration later in the season

The species has adapted readily to human structures. The familiar mud nest is commonly attached beneath bridges, inside barns and sheds, under eaves, and on other sheltered vertical surfaces.

## Identification

**Key features for field diagnosis:**

- **Tail:** distinctly and deeply forked, particularly obvious on adults
- **Wings:** long, narrow, pointed wings producing a fast and graceful silhouette
- **Throat:** reddish or chestnut rather than white
- **Underparts:** buff or warm-toned rather than clean white
- **Upperparts:** dark glossy blue on adults
- **Flight:** fluid, agile, and often low over fields or water
- **Perching:** commonly rests on wires, exposed branches, fences, and structures between feeding flights

The combination of a **rufous throat, warm underparts, and deeply forked tail** is particularly useful when separating Barn Swallow from other Minnesota swallows.

In flight, the long outer tail feathers can make an adult unmistakable. Juveniles require more attention because the tail is shorter and the overall plumage is duller.

## Juvenile Identification

Juvenile Barn Swallows can look substantially different from the long-tailed adults usually pictured in field guides.

Useful juvenile characteristics include:

- **Shorter outer tail feathers:** the fork is present but lacks the exaggerated streamers of an adult
- **Yellow gape:** often conspicuous along the base of the bill
- **Duller upperparts:** less intensely glossy than those of an adult
- **Paler throat and underparts:** warm buff or cinnamon tones without the stronger adult contrast
- **Soft body contour:** fresh juvenile plumage may give the bird a rounder or slightly puffed appearance

These characters are useful in the current photographic sequence, where the yellow gape and relatively short tail are visible in several frames.

## Behavior and Ecology

Barn Swallows are highly aerial insectivores.

Typical behavior includes:

- Pursuing flying insects continuously over open ground and water
- Flying low over fields, ponds, roads, and wetlands
- Making rapid turns and changes of direction while feeding
- Drinking by skimming close to the surface of water
- Resting on exposed branches, wires, fences, and structures between flights
- Preening and stretching extensively while perched
- Gathering mud from wet ground for nest construction

Their short bill can appear surprisingly small when the bird is perched, but the mouth opens broadly to capture insects during flight.

Although a resting swallow often looks almost neckless, stretching or preening can reveal considerably more neck than the normal streamlined posture suggests. Raised wings and displaced contour feathers temporarily break apart the compact shape seen in an undisturbed perched bird.

---

## Nesting

Barn Swallows construct an open cup nest primarily from pellets of mud mixed with grass or other plant material.

Typical nest sites include:

- Barn rafters
- Bridges
- Sheds and outbuildings
- Culverts
- Porches and sheltered ledges
- Other protected structures with an overhead covering

The nest is usually attached to a vertical surface with access to an open flight path.

Adults make repeated trips between wet mud sources and the nest site during construction. Both the nest location and nearby open feeding habitat can make Barn Swallows strongly associated with farms, parks, bridges, and waterfront structures.

## Similar Species

### Tree Swallow

[**Tree Swallow**](/gallery/animals/birds/perching/tree-swallow/) (*Tachycineta bicolor*) is one of the most useful comparisons.

Tree Swallow differs in having:

- Bright blue-green or iridescent upperparts on adults
- Clean white underparts
- No reddish throat
- A moderately forked rather than deeply forked tail
- No long outer tail streamers

Both species have long pointed wings and spend much of their time catching insects in flight. Their perched posture can also be remarkably compact.

The wing-lift photographs of the two species show a similar effect: the neck that seems almost absent in the normal resting posture becomes much more visible when the bird stretches and the contour feathers are disturbed.

### Cliff Swallow

Cliff Swallow differs by:

- A relatively square tail
- Pale or whitish forehead on adults
- Dark throat
- Pale buff rump
- More compact body shape

Its flight silhouette lacks the long forked tail characteristic of Barn Swallow.

### Bank Swallow

Bank Swallow is:

- Brown above rather than blue
- White below with a distinct brown breast band
- Smaller and more plainly marked
- Short-tailed with only a shallow fork

### Northern Rough-winged Swallow

Northern Rough-winged Swallow is:

- Plain gray-brown above
- Dusky white to gray below
- Lacking the strong breast band of Bank Swallow
- Lacking the reddish throat and long forked tail of Barn Swallow

## Field Recognition

Barn Swallows are often easier to recognize by **shape and movement** than by color.

At a distance, watch for:

- Long pointed wings
- A conspicuous forked tail
- Fast but fluid flight
- Low sweeping passes over open ground or water
- Frequent changes of direction while feeding
- Perching on exposed wires or branches between feeding bouts

An adult's tail streamers may remain visible even when plumage color cannot be resolved.

With a juvenile, the shorter tail makes the identification less immediate. The reddish or warm-toned face, buff underparts, pointed wings, and overall swallow structure become more important.
