## Cedar Waxwing — Perspective
{: .taxonomy-heading}

### [*Bombycilla cedrorum*](/field-notes/birds/passeriformes/bombycillidae/bombycilla-cedrorum/)
{: .taxonomy-heading .taxonomy-last}

The [**Cedar Waxwing**](/gallery/animals/birds/perching/cedar-waxwing/) photographs collected here span several encounters and show the species in very different settings: an open branch against blue sky, a spring canopy with fresh buds, a brief courtship exchange, and a high spruce-top perch.

Taken separately, many of the photographs are simple waxwing portraits. Together, they show how much the character of a Cedar Waxwing image can change when the surrounding branches, buds, or evergreen structure remain part of the story.

For identification, habitat, and natural-history details, see the [*Bombycilla cedrorum*](/field-notes/birds/passeriformes/bombycillidae/bombycilla-cedrorum/) species page.

## Open Branch Portraits

The newer open-branch portraits give the Cedar Waxwing page a cleaner gallery lead. **E22A8731** works especially well because it balances a clear view of the bird with enough branch structure to keep the scene anchored. The breast texture is stronger than in the nearby frames, and the bird holds its head more forward rather than turning off to the side.

That head angle was part of the challenge in the field. The bird was visible through a set of limbs, but many frames caught the head turned left. The selected image keeps the waxwing in a natural perch setting while still showing the crest, black mask, yellow belly wash, and smooth overall shape.

**E22A8727** is the stronger field-mark companion. It shows the red waxy wing tips, yellow tail band, black mask, and clean open-sky profile in one frame. With some of the branch structure retained, it feels less isolated than a fully cleaned version and fits better with the surrounding gallery images.

## Spring Bud Exchange

The spring canopy sequence has a different value. It is not as clean as the open-branch portraits, but it carries the stronger behavior story. Two Cedar Waxwings appeared to pass a small green bud or catkin piece back and forth while perched close together in a budding tree.

The repeated green buds around the birds help explain the object being passed. Instead of reading as an isolated berry or unidentified item, it appears tied directly to the tree and the season. That makes the exchange feel more like a small spring moment than a staged portrait.

**E22A2676** is the key image because the birds meet bill-to-bill with the green bud between them. The branches are busy and the birds are partly screened, but that natural screening helps preserve the feeling of watching the behavior unfold from a distance.

## Single-Bird Context

The single-bird frames help separate the bird from the pair behavior and show the classic Cedar Waxwing shape. The crest gives the head a swept-back profile, while the black mask and pale outline around it sharpen the face.

**E22A2670** shows the warm brown head and chest fading into a paler yellow belly and gray lower body. The branch partly hides the lower half, but the long, neat silhouette and yellow tail band still read clearly.

**E22A2668** is less portrait-like, but it adds useful context. The rear view makes the tail band the strongest field mark. The bird is turned away, yet the smooth gray body, pointed crest, folded wing shape, and yellow tail tip keep the identification unmistakable.

**E21A2122** adds personality. The raised crest and open bill turn the same perch into a brief calling moment, giving the page a little more energy after the quieter portraits.

## Photographer’s Perspective

The Cedar Waxwing page changed as the photographs accumulated.

The bud-passing sequence first made the page a behavior story. The branches were busy and the birds were partly screened, but that complexity helped tie the behavior to a real spring tree. Later, the open-branch portraits added a cleaner introduction to the species while preserving enough limb structure to show how the image was found.

E22A8731 now works as the strongest gallery lead because it gives the bird a clear, readable presence without stripping away the surrounding branch context. E22A8727 adds the strongest field-mark companion, especially with the red waxy wing tips and yellow tail band visible. E22A2676 remains the strongest story image because the bill-to-bill exchange gives the page its most distinctive behavior.

The result is less a ranked collection of individual photographs than a progression:

**open portrait → field marks → pair behavior → spring habitat → voice and lookout posture**

That progression is also why the photographs do not need to appear in order of rating. A gallery can tell a stronger story when each image contributes something different.
