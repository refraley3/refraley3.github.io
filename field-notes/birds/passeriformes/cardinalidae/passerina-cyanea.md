## *Passerina cyanea*
{: .taxonomy-heading}

### Family: [Cardinalidae](/field-notes/birds/passeriformes/cardinalidae/)
{: .taxonomy-heading .taxonomy-last}

[**Indigo Bunting**](/gallery/animals/birds/perching/indigo-bunting/) (*Passerina cyanea*) is a small migratory songbird associated with brushy fields, meadow margins, woodland edges, and other areas where low cover meets open space. Breeding males are immediately recognizable in good light by their saturated blue plumage, while females and immature birds are much more subdued and can remain difficult to see among branches and vegetation.

For representative images and visual context, see the page: [**Indigo Bunting**](/gallery/animals/birds/perching/indigo-bunting/) in the **Gallery** section.

---

## Description

*Passerina cyanea* is recognized by:

- **Size:** small, compact songbird, approximately 4.5–5.5 in (12–14 cm)  
- **Breeding male plumage:** deep blue overall, often appearing brighter turquoise or cobalt as the light changes  
- **Female plumage:** warm brown with softer underparts and occasional faint blue in the wings or tail  
- **Bill:** short, thick, and conical, suited for seeds and small insects  
- **Shape:** rounded body, relatively short tail, and compact head  

The male’s blue is especially dependent on viewing angle and light. In shade or at a distance, portions of the plumage may appear nearly black, while direct sunlight can reveal brilliant blue-green highlights.

## Habitat and Range

Typical habitats include:

- Brushy meadow margins  
- Woodland and forest edges  
- Overgrown fields and shrubby openings  
- Hedgerows, roadsides, and utility corridors  

Notes may include:

- A summer breeding species in Minnesota  
- Often found where grasses and low shrubs meet taller trees  
- Males may remain low in brush while also using exposed perches for singing  
- Females and immature birds are more easily overlooked because of their brown plumage and quieter movements  

---

## Identification

**Key features for field diagnosis:**

- **Breeding male:** rich blue plumage without the orange breast of an Eastern Bluebird  
- **Female:** mostly brown, with a compact bunting shape and thick conical bill  
- **Bill:** pale gray to silvery, broad at the base, and sharply pointed  
- **Wings and tail:** may show the strongest blue on females or immature males  
- **Voice:** a bright, paired series of musical phrases, often delivered from an exposed perch  
- **Behavior:** frequently moves between low brush, meadow vegetation, and higher woodland-edge perches  

The combination of small size, compact shape, conical bill, and the male’s blue plumage separates Indigo Bunting from other blue birds commonly encountered in Minnesota.

---

## Behavior and Ecology

- Males often sing from exposed branches overlooking brushy habitat  
- Foraging commonly occurs lower in grasses, shrubs, and weedy vegetation  
- Diet consists of:
  - **Seeds and grains**  
  - **Insects and other small invertebrates**  
  - **Berries and other plant material**  
- Nests are generally placed low in dense vegetation or shrubs  
- During the breeding season, adults may follow repeated routes between feeding cover, song perches, and concealed nesting areas  

---

## Field Observation

A male Indigo Bunting was observed repeatedly moving through the low brush between a grass meadow and the edge of the trees. A brown-plumaged bird, believed to be the female, remained higher in nearby trees. The male occasionally flew toward her position before returning to the underbrush.

The birds were also heard at another nearby location on a separate occasion. Together, these observations suggest a regular movement pattern along the meadow-to-woodland transition. Repeated perches, entry points into the brush, and flight paths between the meadow and tree line may provide better opportunities for future observation and photography.

The male frequently remained partly concealed and increased the distance when approached. Waiting near a known return perch may be more effective than following him through the grass.

---

## Notes

- The male’s color may shift from dark cobalt to bright turquoise depending on sunlight and viewing angle  
- Blue plumage can provide less autofocus contrast than nearby branches, especially when the bird is small in the frame  
- The pale bill, eye line, and boundary between the head and background may provide better focusing targets than the center of the blue breast  
- Often detected by song before the bird becomes visible  
- Knowledge of repeated movement routes may make close observation possible without approaching concealed nesting cover
