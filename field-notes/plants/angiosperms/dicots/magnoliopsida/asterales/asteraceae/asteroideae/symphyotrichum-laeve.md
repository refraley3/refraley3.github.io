[**Smooth Blue Aster**](/gallery/plants/wildflowers/smooth-blue-aster/) (*Symphyotrichum laeve*) is a native perennial wildflower recognized by its open clusters of blue-violet flower heads, smooth or nearly hairless foliage, and upper leaves that clasp the stem.

Another common name is Smooth Aster.

For representative images and visual context, see the page [**Smooth Blue Aster**](/gallery/plants/wildflowers/smooth-blue-aster/) in the **Gallery** section.

## Description

*Symphyotrichum laeve* is recognized by:

- **Growth form:** erect perennial, usually one to three feet tall, arising as one or several stems from the base
- **Stems:** green or reddish, mostly smooth and hairless, sometimes with sparse hairs in the upper portion
- **Leaves:** alternate, firm, smooth or almost waxy, mostly hairless, and toothless or shallowly toothed
- **Leaf attachment:** mid- and upper-stem leaves are stalkless and usually clasp the stem with rounded bases or small ear-like lobes
- **Flower arrangement:** open, branching clusters at the top of the plant and from the upper leaf axils, with stiff branches that generally ascend
- **Flower heads:** usually about one inch across, with approximately 15 to 30 light-blue, blue-violet, or purplish ray flowers
- **Disk flowers:** yellow when fresh, becoming reddish or reddish purple with age
- **Phyllaries:** narrow and arranged in several layers beneath the flower head, appressed to slightly spreading, with darker green lance-shaped to diamond-shaped tips
- **Fruit:** a dry seed with a tuft of pale brown hairs that aids wind dispersal

The smooth, clasping leaves are among the most useful field marks. Flower size and color can vary and overlap with those of other blue-flowered asters, so the leaves, stems, and phyllaries should be considered together.

### Soil and Habitat

Smooth Blue Aster grows in dry to moderately moist, sunny habitats, including prairies, fields, savannas, open woods, woodland edges, and other open ground. It flowers from late summer into autumn, generally from August through October in Minnesota.

The photographed plants were found in Minnesota among meadow vegetation. One encounter produced a close, dew-covered portrait of an individual flower. A later observation showed a more openly branching plant with smaller-appearing flower heads, multiple stages of bloom, and foliage extending along the stems.

---

### Similar Species

The most important comparison is Sky-blue Aster (*Symphyotrichum oolentangiense*), which grows in similar dry, open habitats and blooms during the same part of the season.

The two species are best separated by a combination of leaf texture, leaf attachment, flower size, and stem texture:

- **Smooth Blue Aster:** leaves smooth or almost waxy, with mid- and upper leaves generally clasping the stem; flowers usually about one inch across; stems mostly smooth
- **Sky-blue Aster:** leaves rough like fine sandpaper and generally narrowing into winged stalks rather than clasping the stem; flowers commonly one-half to three-quarters of an inch across; upper stems often rough-textured

Flower size alone is not decisive. A close macro portrait can make an individual flower appear substantially larger than flowers shown within a branching cluster. The attachment of several mid-stem leaves and the texture of the leaves and stem provide stronger evidence.

New England Aster (*Symphyotrichum novae-angliae*) may also have blue-violet to purple flower heads, but it usually has many more, narrower rays and conspicuously hairy or glandular stems and phyllaries.

### Ecological Notes

The flower heads attract bees, butterflies, flies, and other late-season pollinators. The branching clusters may carry buds, newly opened flowers, mature yellow disks, and aging reddish disks at the same time, extending the period during which the plant offers floral resources.

The photographs also show how flowers function as hunting sites. In E22A9503, a pale crab spider waits among the dew droplets against the face of the aster, where its coloration makes it difficult to distinguish from the moisture on the rays.

After flowering, the dry seeds develop tufts of hair that allow them to disperse on the wind. The perennial plant returns from an underground crown and rhizome.

### Field Observations

The first photographed flower became “Morning Jewels.” Dew collected along the blue-violet rays and emphasized the folds, irregular edges, and changing colors of the disk. Seen alone at close range, the flower appeared unusually large—perhaps much larger than the roughly one-inch heads observed later.

D79A5416 from that encounter was posted to iNaturalist and identified as Smooth Blue Aster. The later E22A9505-16 observation was posted more cautiously as American asters (*Symphyotrichum*) because its smaller flower heads and open branching habit initially suggested Sky-blue Aster.

The later visit produced a more useful botanical sequence. E22A9505-16 shows tightly closed buds, opening heads, fresh yellow disks, and aging disks together along the branching stem. E22A9503 adds a less obvious detail: a crab spider nearly indistinguishable from the dew. E22A9530 was made specifically to document the leaves and their attachment to the stem.

The newer observation remains a working identification until the leaf texture, attachment, phyllaries, and iNaturalist response can be considered together. If it is confirmed as Sky-blue Aster, those photographs should be separated from the confirmed Smooth Blue Aster observation rather than used as evidence for *Symphyotrichum laeve*.
