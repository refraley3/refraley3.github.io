{: .taxonomy-heading .taxonomy-last}

The [**Golden-eye Lichen**](/gallery/fungi/lichens/golden-eye/) photographs collected here span several observations of *Teloschistes chrysophthalmus*. Rather than following a single encounter, the sequence compares the same species under different photographic conditions: warm natural sunlight, direct flash, a mixed lichen community, and a wet high-magnification focus stack. ## Natural Light Across the Branch

**E22A2623** establishes the colony within a lichen-covered branch as sunlight begins to reach the subject. The wider frame shows the Golden-eye as only one element in a crowded bark community, surrounded by gray foliose lichens and smaller yellow growths. The soft background and unlit portions of the branch preserve the sense of waiting for a moving patch of sunlight.

**E22A2624** moves closer after the light reaches the colony. The orange apothecia brighten first, while curled lobes and cilia fall into softer shadow. The more directional illumination gives the tuft greater depth than the flash-lit views and separates its amber-orange tones from the cooler gray lichens around it.

## Flash and Structure

The flash-lit photographs trade some of the warmth and dimensionality of natural light for a more explicit view of structure. **E22A2614** resolves the dense marginal cilia, curled lobes, and numerous orange apothecia across the top of the colony. The even illumination also makes the surrounding gray lichens easier to compare within the same plane.

**E22A2611** steps back enough to show the Golden-eye as a compact tuft rising from the bark. From this angle the fruticose growth form is especially clear: the colony is not an appressed rosette but a tangled, shrubby mass projecting above neighboring foliose lichens.

## Earlier Studies and Community Context

**E22A0106** isolates a compact colony on a slender birch twig. Warm light concentrates attention on the crowded orange apothecia while the subdued bark and background keep the tuft visually separate from its surroundings. The angle also shows how irregular lobes lift away from the branch and create a miniature, layered landscape.

**E22A0184_85_88** expands the context. The Golden-eye shares the twig with gray Physcia-like rosettes and small yellow crusts, making the photograph as much a record of a lichen community as a portrait of one species. The bright orange tuft remains the visual center, but its scale becomes clearer when compared with the surrounding growths.

## Field Characters Across the Sequence

The photographs record complementary visual characters of *Teloschistes chrysophthalmus*:

* **E22A2624** shows the warm orange apothecia, curled lobes, and ciliate margins under low natural sunlight.
* **E22A2614** gives the clearest flash-lit view of the dense cilia and tangled branching structure.
* **E22A2611** emphasizes the compact, shrubby growth form rising above the bark.
* **E22A0184_85_88** places the species among neighboring foliose and crustose lichens and helps establish its scale.
* **E22A9460** shows the marked change in color and surface character when wet and at higher magnification.

These images are photographic observations rather than a complete diagnostic series. For identification characters, comparison with similar lichens, and notes on the species, see the [*Teloschistes chrysophthalmus*](/field-notes/lichen/teloschistes-chrysophthalmus/) species page.

## Perspective

Natural sunlight gives the dry colony its strongest dimensionality; flash clarifies cilia and branching; wider views place the tuft among neighboring lichens; and the high-magnification view from a humid, foggy morning pushes close enough to make individual apothecia dominate the frame.

**mixed branch in moving light → warm natural-light portrait → flash-lit structure → twig community → humid morning at high magnification**
{: .caption}

Across those changes, the orange apothecia remain the most persistent visual feature. The photographs also show why a lichen can look markedly different from one observation to the next: moisture, angle, illumination, and magnification alter color and texture without changing the underlying organism.
