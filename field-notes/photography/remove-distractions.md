Removing distractions is one of the most effective ways to improve a nature photograph. Small branches, bright highlights, stray leaves, and out-of-focus objects can draw attention away from the subject. Photoshop offers several tools for cleaning these distractions while maintaining a natural appearance.

The workflow below reflects my standard editing process for photographs intended for personal display and publication on this website.

> **Ethics Note**
>
> The edits described here are intended for artistic presentation. Many photography contests, exhibitions, and scientific publications prohibit AI-generated content or significant removal of natural elements. Always review the rules before editing an image for competition or documentary purposes.

---

## Before You Begin

Always preserve the original photograph.

Create a working copy before editing.

Suggested folder structure:

```
Original/
Working/
Print/
Web/
```

Open the working copy in Photoshop.

---

## 1. Evaluate the Image

Before selecting any tools, determine what actually distracts from the subject.

Common distractions include:

- Bright leaves
- Twigs crossing the subject
- Small branches
- Out-of-focus foreground objects
- Bright patches of sky
- Sensor dust
- Small insects
- Background clutter

Ask:

> Does this element contribute to the story, or does it simply attract attention?

Not every imperfection needs to be removed.

---

## 2. Work Non-Destructively

Duplicate the Background layer.

**Layer → Duplicate Layer**

Rename it:

- Cleanup
- Background Cleanup

Avoid editing the original layer whenever possible.

---

## 3. Start with AI-Assisted Removal

For many modern Photoshop edits, the **Remove Tool** provides an excellent starting point.

Select:

**Remove Tool**

Enable:

- Remove After Each Stroke
- Sample All Layers (if working on a blank cleanup layer)

Paint over the distraction.

Photoshop analyzes surrounding texture and replaces the selected object using Adobe's AI-assisted removal.

This method works well for:

- Thin branches
- Small leaves
- Grass stems
- Background debris
- Sensor dust
- Small bright highlights

---

## 4. Review the Result

Never assume the first AI result is correct.

Zoom to:

- 100%
- 200%

Look for:

- Repeating textures
- Smearing
- Soft edges
- Artificial patterns
- Missing detail

If necessary:

Undo and try a shorter brush stroke.

Several small removals usually produce better results than one large selection.

---

## 5. Refine with the Healing Brush

Some areas benefit from traditional repair.

Use:

- Healing Brush
- Spot Healing Brush

These tools preserve surrounding texture while blending color and brightness.

Useful for:

- Bark
- Rock
- Smooth leaves
- Soft backgrounds

---

## 6. Use the Clone Stamp When Needed

The Clone Stamp provides complete control.

Use it when AI introduces visible artifacts.

Best practices:

- Soft brush
- Low opacity
- Frequently resample
- Clone from nearby areas

Ideal for:

- Tree bark
- Repeating textures
- Fine branches
- Detailed backgrounds

---

## 7. Preserve Natural Edges

Be especially careful around:

- Feathers
- Fur
- Insect legs
- Antennae
- Spider silk
- Leaf edges

Avoid painting across these details.

Instead, work around the subject with small brush strokes.

---

## 8. Reduce Rather Than Remove

Sometimes a distraction only needs less emphasis.

Instead of deleting it completely, consider:

- Darkening it
- Lowering saturation
- Softening contrast

Adjustment Layers often produce a more natural result than complete removal.

---

## 9. Crop Before Heavy Editing

A small crop may eliminate distractions with no retouching at all.

Consider:

- Tightening the composition
- Changing aspect ratio
- Removing empty edges

The simplest solution is often the best.

---

## 10. Inspect the Entire Image

Review the image at several zoom levels.

- Fit on Screen
- 50%
- 100%
- 200%

Look for:

- Repeating patterns
- Blotches
- Halos
- Obvious cloning
- Texture mismatches

If an edit becomes noticeable, simplify it.

The goal is for viewers to notice the photograph—not the editing.

---

## Contest and Documentary Editing

Photography competitions often have stricter standards than personal artistic work.

Many contests prohibit:

- AI-generated replacements
- Generative Fill
- Content-Aware expansion
- Removal of natural objects
- Addition of objects
- Composite images

Some competitions allow only:

- Exposure adjustments
- White balance
- Contrast
- Cropping
- Dust spot removal
- Global sharpening

Always read the contest rules before editing.

If there is any doubt, preserve the original RAW file and maintain an untouched master image for verification.

---

## My Typical Workflow

For photographs published on this website, my editing usually follows this order:

1. Process the RAW image.
2. Crop the composition.
3. Remove distracting background elements using the Remove Tool.
4. Refine with Healing Brush or Clone Stamp when needed.
5. Adjust brightness and color.
6. Apply sharpening.
7. Save a layered PSD.
8. Export separate JPEG files for the website and printing.

---

## Tips

- Keep edits believable.
- Zoom in frequently.
- Save layered PSD files.
- Work in small sections.
- Use several light corrections instead of one aggressive edit.
- Preserve the character of the habitat whenever possible.
- When preparing images for contests, avoid AI-assisted removal unless the rules explicitly permit it.
